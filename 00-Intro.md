# Fit, Flow, Fidelity



## Digital Craftsmanship Through a Disabled Lens



### Intro



This is not *only* about accessibility. It is about quality. It is about effort. It is about trust. It is about what becomes visible when we look at digital products through disabled experience instead of treating disability as an edge case, a compliance obligation, or a late-stage review.



For years, our industry has had a familiar way of talking about accessibility. We ask whether something passes. Does it meet the standard? Does it satisfy the requirement? Does it work with a screen reader? Can it be used with a keyboard? Is the contrast high enough? Are captions available? Is the bug closed?



Those necessary questions matter. I do not want to diminish them. Standards matter. Testing matters. Compliance matters. A product that fails the basics can block people outright, and blocked access is not a matter of taste or polish. It is exclusion.



But passing the basics is not the same as being well made.



A product can meet a requirement and still feel fragile. It can technically support a task and still make the task exhausting. It can expose a control and still make the user guess what state they are in. It can offer customization and then forget the setup someone depends on. It can provide an accessibility feature and still make that feature feel like another tool the user has to manage.



That is the gap I hope to talk about.
Accessibility gets someone through the door. Craft determines whether they can stay.



Craft is not decoration. It is not visual polish added at the end. It is not the extra delight that comes after the serious work is done. In digital systems, craft is the way a product carries meaning, preserves effort, and earns trust through repeated use.



For disabled users, the difference between access and craft can be immediate.



If you rely on magnification, unclear layout is not just messy. It changes whether you can orient yourself. If you use a screen reader, a visual state change that is not announced is not just a small miss. It removes evidence you need to act. If you use voice, switch access, keyboard navigation, eye gaze, or adaptive input, inconsistent focus is not just a rough edge. It changes whether the next command will land where you expect.



If you manage pain, fatigue, cognitive load, sensory sensitivity, low vision, hearing loss, tremor, fluctuating attention, or limited reach, small amounts of friction do not stay small. They accumulate.



The extra click matters. The hidden setting matters. The prompt that appears at the wrong time matters. The control that moves matters. The update that resets something matters. The feature that helps in theory but requires constant setup matters.



These things may not always stop the task. That is part of why they are easy to underestimate.



A barrier blocks you. *Friction drains you*.



And drained users do not always file bugs. They do not always send feedback that says, “This product technically works, but it costs too much of my attention, patience, vision, memory, coordination, or stamina.” They adapt. They work around. They avoid. They ask someone else. They stop trusting the product. Sometimes they stop using it altogether.



When teams look only for failure, they can miss this quieter cost.



That is why we need another language for product quality. Not a replacement for accessibility standards. Not a softer version of compliance. Not a new checklist. A lens.



Fit. Flow. Fidelity.



#### Fit asks whether the system gives people confidence without guesswork.



Do I know what is happening? Do I know what will happen next? Do I know how to recover if something goes wrong? Fit is about state, consequence, and recovery. It is about whether the system is legible enough that the user can act without having to inspect the interface like a detective.



#### Flow asks whether the system protects momentum without fatigue.



Is my energy going toward the goal, or toward managing the interface around the goal? Flow is about repeated mechanics, context switching, interruption, input effort, and recovery after distraction. It is not about making people faster in ideal conditions. It is about helping people continue in real ones.



#### Fidelity asks whether the system earns trust over time.



Can I rely on this tomorrow? Will my settings persist? Will my learned behavior still matter? Will this work across sessions, devices, updates, contexts, and failure states? Fidelity is about the promises a system makes and whether it keeps them.



Together, these three lenses help explain why accessibility can technically exist and still not feel like care.



A caption feature may make speech perceivable, but if it is buried in an interface, it may fail Flow. A setting may be available, but if it resets after an update, it fails Fidelity. A control may be visible, but if its state is unclear or not exposed to assistive technology, it fails Fit. A system may offer help, but if using the help becomes its own task, the craft is still incomplete.



The point is not that every product should remove all effort. Some friction is useful. Some actions should slow us down. Security should sometimes ask for confirmation. Destructive actions should make us pause. Administrative tools should not make high-impact changes feel casual.



The question is whether the effort serves the user’s goal, protects them from harm, or supports understanding — or whether it simply leaks energy.



An account login can fail Fit when the user does not know which identity is active. It can fail Flow when authentication interrupts the task it was supposed to protect. It can fail Fidelity when the recovery method that worked yesterday does not survive getting a new phone.



A spreadsheet can fail Fit when structure is only visual. It can fail Flow when analysis becomes navigation labor. It can fail Fidelity when the workbook only makes sense to the person who built it.



A meeting can offer captions and still fail participation. A security prompt can protect the organization and still train people to ignore it. An admin tool can be powerful and still leave the user unsure which policy applied, which portal is authoritative, or what changed after an update.



These are not only accessibility issues in the narrow sense. They are quality issues revealed through accessibility.



**That distinction matters.**



Disabled people are often treated as special cases in product work. Their needs are separated, documented, tested, and triaged. The disabled experience does not only reveal where products fail disabled users, it reveals **where systems are overconfident about everyone**.



Unclear state is bad design. Disabled users may feel it first.



Repeated setup is bad design. Disabled users may pay for it more often.



Fragile customization is bad design. Disabled users may depend on it more completely.



Inaccessible recovery is bad design. Disabled users may have fewer alternate paths when things break.



The failure is not that disabled users are difficult to design for. The failure is that many systems are built with too little margin for human variation.



I hope this work can help people take that variation seriously.



They do not replace standards. They help us see what standards alone may not describe. They give teams a way to talk about the felt quality of access: whether a system is clear enough to trust, light enough to continue using, and dependable enough to become part of someone’s life.



That is the kind of craftsmanship this work is asking for.



Not perfection. Not polish for its own sake.



Not accessibility as accommodation layered on after the real product is already done.



Craft that understands the cost of uncertainty, that respects energy as a limited resource, that treats user choices as promises. Craft that recognizes disabled experience as valuable, vital for great products.



* Fit shows where confidence breaks.
* Flow shows where energy drains.
* Fidelity shows where trust erodes.



And when we design through those lenses, accessibility stops feeling like a minimum requirement. It starts feeling like the work of making digital products worthy for us.
